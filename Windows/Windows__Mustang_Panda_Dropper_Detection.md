# APT: Mustang Panda Dropper Activity Detected

## Description
Alert generated by the `APT: Mustang Panda Dropper Activity Detected` detection. Detects process parameters and command patterns specific to droppers used by the Mustang Panda group (also known as TA416 or RedDelta). The rule identifies the use of obfuscated environment variables, the creation of scheduled tasks with decoy names such as 'Security Script', and the execution of VBScript from text files - tactics designed to evade standard monitoring solutions. The alert must be reviewed by the SOC to validate its legitimacy, scope, affected asset, and potential impact.

## Objective
Detect and prioritize potential malicious, anomalous, or unauthorized activity related to Windows host, enabling the SOC to initiate a structured investigation, reduce operational risk, and activate containment procedures when indicators of compromise are identified.

## Logic
The detection is triggered by the `APT: Mustang Panda Dropper Activity Detected` rule. The prioritization logic should consider severity, asset criticality, event recurrence, network source/destination, involved identity, platform, and correlated activity in supporting data sources.

Patterns or conditions to validate:

- Exact match of the detection name/signature with this use case.
- Severity equal to or above the SOC-defined threshold.
- Production, critical, publicly exposed, or sensitive-data asset.
- IP address, domain, user, process, or resource not recognized by the asset/account owner.
- Logs or telemetry from the supporting data sources occurring close to the first or last observed event.
- Sensitive changes following the event, such as permission changes, new persistence, credential use, logging changes, public exposure, command execution, or data access.

## Investigation

Mandatory 5W Questions:

- **Who:** Which user, host, service account, process, or external actor is involved?
- **What:** What action was observed, and what asset was affected? Was there access, execution, a configuration change, network communication, or an authentication attempt?
- **When:** When did the first and last observations occur? Did the activity take place outside expected business hours or near another change?
- **Where:** From which host, IP address, country, ASN, or platform did the activity originate?
- **Why:** Is there an operational justification, approved automation, authorized testing activity, or planned change that explains the event?

## Detailed Checklist for Analysts Reviewing the Alert

- Detection/rule name, severity, affected host, user, or resource, and the platform/category it was raised on.
- Involved identity: username, service account, process owner, or the external source identified in the alert.
- Source and destination details available for this alert (IP, domain, port, protocol, country, ASN) where applicable.
- First and last observed event, frequency, and recurrence within the analysis period.
- Sensitive follow-on actions, such as privilege changes, new persistence mechanisms, log tampering, or data access.
- Relationship with other alerts, EDR detections, SIEM correlations, or existing tickets for the same host/user.
- Confirmation with the owner regarding expected activity, approved automation, authorized testing, or unknown behavior.
- Specific fields referenced by this detection's own alert template:
  - `host` - the affected host
  - `command` - the command observed
  - `process` - the process involved

## Evidence for Determination

### False Positives

- The `APT: Mustang Panda Dropper Activity Detected` rule has not yet been tuned to this environment and is matching a legitimate, documented pattern - confirm with the asset/account owner before escalating.
- A legitimate debugging, monitoring, or anti-tampering tool triggered the same low-level behavior this detection watches for.
- An approved administrative action (patching, reconfiguration) incidentally matches the detection's pattern.
- The activity reflects the organization's own, legitimate infrastructure or domain/certificate procurement.
- A security or threat-intelligence team intentionally registered look-alike infrastructure for defensive research.

### True Positives

- The rule identifies the use of obfuscated environment variables, the creation of scheduled tasks with decoy names such as 'Security Script', and the execution of VBScript from text files - tactics designed to evade standard monitoring solutions.
- The activity has no legitimate operational explanation and matches a known defense-evasion or anti-analysis technique.
- It coincides with other indicators of compromise on the same host or account.
- The responsible user or process cannot be identified or does not explain the activity when contacted.
- The newly acquired infrastructure (domain, VPS, certificate) has no legitimate business relationship with the organization and later correlates with phishing or C2 activity observed elsewhere.
- Threat-intelligence enrichment links the infrastructure to a known actor or campaign.

### True Negatives

- The behavior is confirmed to come from an approved, documented tool or maintenance action.
- The resource is confirmed as an approved, internally procured asset.

### False Negatives

- The adversary uses a technique variant or tooling this detection's specific logic does not yet cover.
- The evasive action is brief enough that it completes before generating a strong enough signal.
- The actor acquires infrastructure through a reseller, proxy, or compromised third-party account that doesn't surface in standard registration/threat-intel feeds.
